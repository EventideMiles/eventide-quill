# Chapter 3: The Relic

The traveller did not leave that night. She told herself it was because the roads would be watched, and that was true, but it was also true that she could not bring herself to go, and so she stayed, in a narrow guest room at the top of the scholar's tall house, and she lay awake in the dark and listened to the city settling around her. Somewhere a dog was barking, and somewhere else a bell was tolling, and the sounds wove together into a kind of tapestry that was neither pleasant nor unpleasant but simply there, the ordinary music of a place she did not yet know.

In the morning the scholar was different. She moved more quickly, and her voice had lost its careful evenness, and she spoke of arrangements as though they had already been made. A horse would be saddled. A cloak would be provided. A map, marked in the scholar's own hand, would be placed in the traveller's saddlebag before she set out. The traveller listened to all of this and nodded and said very little, and the scholar did not seem to notice the silence, or if she noticed it she did not remark upon it.

"You will ride north," the scholar said. "Three days, perhaps four, depending on the weather and the road. At the end of it you will find a village that is not marked on any map you have seen, and in the village you will find a house with a red door, and behind the red door you will find the one I have spoken of. Give her the relic. Tell her my name. Tell her also that the order is awake, and that what was hidden will not stay hidden much longer."

"And then?"

"And then you may do as you please. I would suggest, gently, that you go home, if you have a home to go to. Many people, these days, do not."

The traveller took the relic in its cloth and held it for a long moment. It was lighter than she remembered, and cooler, and she had the curious sensation that it was listening to her, or perhaps that it had always been listening, and that her entire journey had been a conversation conducted in silence. She wrapped it again and placed it in her bag, and the scholar watched her do this and said nothing.

At the door the old woman pressed a parcel of bread and cheese into her hands, and a small leather bottle of something that smelled of herbs. "For the road," the woman said, and her creased face softened for just a moment into something almost like kindness. The traveller thanked her, and meant it, and the boy in the grey coat was waiting in the street below with two horses, and the morning was grey and the cobblestones were wet, and she had the strange and very clear feeling that she was setting out upon a road from which she would not return.

She mounted. The scholar stood in the doorway and raised one hand, and the traveller raised hers in answer, and then the boy turned his horse and she followed him, and they clattered down the long hill toward the harbour gate, and the city opened before them, grey and vast and indifferent, and somewhere behind her, in a tall narrow house, an old woman closed the door.

She did not look back. There was, she had decided, ultimately, nothing to look back for. The road lay ahead, and the relic lay against her hip, and the morning, for all its greyness, was hers. She rode out through the gate, and the dog was still barking somewhere in the streets behind her, and the bell was still tolling, and the world, very quietly, and without any ceremony at all, continued to turn.
