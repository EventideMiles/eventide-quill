# Chapter 1: The Harbour

The ship was carried into the harbour by a sluggish grey tide, and the traveller stood very still at the rail, watching the city unfold before her like a tapestry of weathered rooftops and chimney smoke. It was, she told herself, quite beautiful in its own rather shabby way, and she felt suddenly afraid of what she would find there. Perhaps the scholar would not see her. Perhaps the order had already moved against her. Perhaps the relic was gone entirely.

She descended the gangplank swiftly, her boots striking the wet stones, and the wind tugged at her cloak with cold fingers. The harbour was a bustling place — she had been told this many times — and yet the crowds that morning were thin and watchful, as though something extraordinary had happened in the night and no one wished to speak of it. A beggar whispered her name as she passed, or something that sounded very like it, and she walked faster, her heart beating quickly, her thoughts turning over and over the same worried questions until she could bear them no longer. In conclusion, she decided, the city was not what she had imagined, and she was not prepared for any of it.

A boy in a grey coat was waiting at the foot of the steps. He did not say anything. He merely inclined his head, gently, and turned, and she followed him up the long hill toward the quarter where the scholars kept their tall, quiet houses. The streets were steep, and the morning light caught the windows and made them flicker like the surface of a deep and unreadable river. She was tired. She was, if she was honest with herself, very tired, and she had not slept properly since the crossing began.

"Why have you come?" the boy asked, without turning.

She considered lying. She considered, briefly, telling him the whole truth: that she was here to find the scholar, that she carried a sealed letter, that the relic was not what people believed it to be. Instead she murmured something about family business, and the boy accepted it with a small nod, and they walked on in silence, and the silence grew between them like a third traveller, heavy and unwelcome.

The scholar's house was reached at last, a tall narrow building pressed between two larger ones, its door painted black and its windows shuttered. A lantern had been lit above the step, though it was full morning, and the boy knocked twice and stepped back. The door was opened by an old woman whose face was creased like a folded map, and she looked at the traveller for a long moment before speaking.

"You are expected," she said, and her voice was surprisingly gentle for so stern a face. "Come in. The scholar will see you in the parlour."

The parlour was dark and smelled of old paper and cold tea. The scholar sat by the window with her back to the light, and the traveller could not make out her features at first, only the shape of her, the stillness, the careful way her hands rested on the arms of the chair. The relic lay on the table between them, wrapped in cloth, and the traveller understood, with a sinking feeling, that the scholar already knew everything.

"Sit," the scholar said. "We have much to discuss, and very little time to discuss it. The order is moving, and they will not be turned aside by ordinary means."
