# Chapter 2: The Scholar

The scholar did not look up when the traveller sat down. She continued to study the relic, turning it slowly in her long fingers, and the silence in the parlour grew until it became almost a living thing, pressing against the walls and the shuttered windows. The traveller, who had journeyed a very great distance to reach this room, found that she had no notion of how to begin, and so she sat, and she waited, and she let the old woman pour her a cup of cold tea that she did not drink.

"You have questions," the scholar said at last, and her voice was low and even, like the surface of a still pond. "Perhaps you should ask them. Perhaps, however, you already know the answers, and you are merely afraid to hear them spoken aloud."

"I am not afraid," the traveller said, and immediately regretted it, for it was clearly a lie and they both knew it.

The scholar smiled, faintly, and the smile did not reach her eyes. She set the relic down upon the cloth and folded her hands above it, and the relic, for its part, did nothing, which was in its own way the most alarming thing of all. The traveller had expected light, or warmth, or some small sign that the thing was more than an old shape wrapped in older linen. She had not expected indifference.

"Tell me what you know of the order," the scholar said.

The traveller told her. She told her of the harbour, and the silent crowds, and the boy who had guided her up the hill without speaking. She told her of the beggar who had whispered her name. She told her, hesitantly, of the letter she carried, which she had not yet opened and which she was beginning to suspect she would never open. The scholar listened without moving, her hands still folded, her eyes still fixed upon the relic, and when the traveller had finished there was another long silence.

"You do not understand what you have brought me," the scholar said.

"I understand that it is important," the traveller said, rather sharply. "I understand that I was chosen to carry it. I understand that the order wants it back, and that they will, in all probability, kill us both to reclaim it."

"You use the word understand very loosely," the scholar said. "You do not understand at all. What you carry is not a relic in any sense you would recognise. It is a key — and the door it opens is not one that should be opened, not by the order, not by me, and certainly not by a frightened young woman who arrived in this city this very morning on a leaking ferry."

The traveller opened her mouth to reply, and then closed it again, for there was nothing to say. The scholar was right. The scholar was, she suspected, almost always right, and the traveller felt a slow anger rising in her throat, hot and bitter, mingled with a shame she could not quite name. She had walked bravely across half a country to deliver this thing, and now she was being told, in effect, that her courage had been wasted.

"What would you have me do?" she asked, and her voice was steadier than she felt.

The scholar rose, and crossed to the window, and opened the shutters a crack. A thin blade of morning light fell across the floor. "I would have you leave this city tonight," she said. "I would have you take the relic with you, and I would have you go to a place I will name, and give it into the keeping of one who is older than I am. Ultimately, that is the only course that remains to us. Anything else is delay, and we have delayed long enough."

"And if I refuse?"

The scholar turned, and for the first time the traveller saw her face clearly, and it was the face of a woman who had not slept for a very long while. "Then we are all of us lost," she said. "You, and I, and very likely the city itself."
